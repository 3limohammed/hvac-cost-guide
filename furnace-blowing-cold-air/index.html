Why Is My Furnace Blowing Cold Air? (7 Causes, Fixes & Costs)

✓ Diagnostic Benchmark DIY Solutions: $0 – $35 Typical Pro Repairs: $120 – $650

Quick Answer: If your furnace is blowing cold air, check if the thermostat fan is set to ON instead of AUTO (which circulates unheated air continuously). The next most common culprit is a dirty air filter causing the furnace to overheat and trip the high-limit switch, or a dirty flame sensor ($100–$250) that shuts the burners off 5 seconds after ignition.

Critical Gas Safety Warning

If you smell natural gas (a rotten egg or sulfur odor) or your carbon monoxide detector alarms, do not flip any electrical switches or attempt to troubleshoot your furnace. Evacuate the home immediately, leave the door open, and call your local gas utility provider or 911 from outside.

The 7 Most Common Causes of Cold Air from a Furnace

1. Thermostat Fan Set to "ON" Instead of "AUTO"

Just like air conditioners, furnaces have a dedicated fan setting. When set to ON, the indoor blower fan runs continuously 24 hours a day. When the gas burners finish heating the heat exchanger and turn off, the fan keeps pushing unheated room-temperature air through your supply registers, making rooms feel drafty and cold.

The Fix: Switch the fan mode to AUTO so the blower only activates when the burners are producing active heat.

2. Dirty Flame Sensor (Burners Shut Off After 5 Seconds)

The flame sensor is a small, bent metal rod positioned directly in the flame path of the burner assembly. Its job is safety: it detects whether a flame is present through a process called flame rectification (measuring a tiny electrical current of 1.5 to 5.0 microamps through the ionized flame).

Over several heating seasons, carbon deposits and airborne silica coat the rod. When the gas valve opens, the igniter lights the flame, but the coated sensor fails to register the microamp signal. For safety, the control board assumes gas is leaking unburned and closes the gas valve within 3 to 7 seconds.

The Fix: Shut off electrical power to the furnace. Remove the 1/4-inch hex screw holding the flame sensor, and gently buff the metal rod clean using fine emery cloth or an abrasive scotch-brite pad. Never use sandpaper, which leaves silicon residue.

3. Clogged Air Filter & Tripped High-Limit Switch

Your furnace's heat exchanger operates at internal temperatures between 140°F and 170°F. The blower fan continuously pulls cool return air across the metal heat exchanger to carry heat away into your living spaces.

If the air filter is choked with dust, pet hair, or debris, airflow plummets. Heat builds up rapidly inside the heat exchanger cabinet until it exceeds safe limits (typically 200°F). The high-limit safety switch trips, immediately shutting off the gas valve to prevent the heat exchanger from cracking. However, the blower fan continues running to cool the metal, blowing cold air into your home.

4. Cracked Hot Surface Igniter (HSI)

Modern furnaces use an electronic silicon carbide or silicon nitride hot surface igniter that glows white-hot (over 2,000°F) to ignite the gas stream. Igniters are brittle and have an average lifespan of 4 to 7 years.

When an igniter cracks or reaches the end of its life, it cannot glow. The furnace will attempt ignition: you will hear the inducer motor spin up, followed by a click from the gas valve, but no flame will appear. Replacing a hot surface igniter costs $150 to $360.

5. Clogged Condensate Drain on High-Efficiency Furnaces

If your furnace exhausts through white PVC pipes rather than metal venting, you own a 90%+ AFUE condensing furnace. These units extract so much heat from combustion gases that water vapor condenses into liquid water (producing 1 to 3 gallons of acidic condensate per day).

If the plastic drain line, trap, or condensate pump clogs with bacterial slime or freezes during extreme cold, water backs up into the secondary heat exchanger or collector box. A safety pressure switch trips, locking out the burners while leaving the blower running.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my furnace blowing cold air when the heat is on?

The most common culprit is a dirty air filter that causes the furnace to overheat and trip the high-limit safety switch, or a carbon-coated flame sensor that cannot sense the burner flame and shuts down the gas valve after 5 seconds.

How do I reset my furnace?

Turn the thermostat to OFF. Locate the power switch (which looks like a light switch mounted on the side of the furnace or nearby wall) and flip it to OFF for 60 seconds, then flip it back to ON and return the thermostat to HEAT mode.

How much does it cost to fix a furnace blowing cold air?

DIY fixes like filter replacements cost $15 to $35. Professional repairs range from $100 to $250 for flame sensor cleaning/replacement, $150 to $360 for a hot surface igniter, $180 to $380 for a limit switch, or $350 to $700 for a control board.
